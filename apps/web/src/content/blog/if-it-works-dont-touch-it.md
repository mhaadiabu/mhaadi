---
title: if it works, don't touch it
date: 'July 19, 2026'
description: 'a half-baked truth about software engineering'
time: 2
---

one thing every software engineer has heard at least once in their career is "_if it works, don't touch it_". this phrase is often used to discourage developers from making changes to a system that is functioning correctly, even if there are potential improvements that could be made.
getting into the field, i never really had anyone to guide me through the nuances of software engineering. i was left to tinker and figure things out on my own, for the 6 years i spent learning and making stuff with vanilla javascript, html, and css (and a little bit of jQuery later on). it wasn't until 3 years ago that i got into react and typescript, and even then, i had never heard of the phrase "if it works, don't touch it". i always learnt to improve and refactor code, and to always try to make things better, which in my opinion is the most effective way to learn and grow as a software engineer.

i wouldn't necessarily call the phrase is wrong, but it is a half-baked truth. while it is true that making changes to a system that is functioning correctly can introduce new bugs and issues, it is also true that not making changes can lead to stagnation and missed opportunities for improvement.
i'm someone who is not always satisfied with leaving things as they are, and i believe that there is always room for improvement. sometimes that means touching things inappropriately and learning from the aftermath of it.

the best way i have found to learn in this field is to make mistakes, break things and mend them. this is how i have learned the most, and it has helped me to become a better software engineer. by making mistakes and learning from them, i have been able to improve my skills and knowledge, and to become more confident in my abilities. breaking everything apart and trying to figure out if there are different ways to put them back together helps you understand how things work, and how to make them work better. while that might not always be the case, you end up learning why things are the way they are when you do run into those exceptions.

useful as it might be to stay on the safer side and leaving sleeping dogs to lie, i believe that it is important to take risks and to challenge the status quo. by doing so, we can learn and grow as software engineers, and we can make meaningful contributions to the field. while it is important to be mindful of the potential risks and consequences of making changes, it is also important to be willing to take calculated risks and to learn from our mistakes.

my takeaway from this is:
_even if it works, you should probably touch it, but do so with caution and a willingness to learn from the experience_.
